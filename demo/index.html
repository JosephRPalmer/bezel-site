The matriarchs of the open sea

In orca families the oldest females lead. Long after their last calf, their memory of where the fish are may be what keeps everyone fed.

Words by the Pelagic science desk · Illustrations by Pelagic studio · 9 min read

The pod surfaces in a line, one breath after another, the tall fins of the males rising highest. But the animal setting the course is the smallest fin of all: an old female, swimming a little ahead, choosing the turn the others will follow.

Orcas live in tight family groups built around their mothers. Sons and daughters often stay with her for life, and when food is scarce, it is the older females who tend to lead the way to where it can be found.

“She is the map. When the salmon runs fail, the family follows the one who remembers the last bad year.”

The science of orca grandmothers

Where the food is. Kelp forests shelter the fish many orcas hunt; krill feed the great baleen whales; moon jellyfish drift through both.

Orcas and humans are among the very few animals known to go through menopause. One idea is that a grandmother does more for her family by guiding it than by raising more calves of her own, and studies of long-watched populations suggest the young are more likely to survive when she is alive.

Her knowledge is cultural: different populations have different calls, different prey and different ways of hunting, passed from one generation to the next. Lose the oldest animals and some of that knowledge may go with them.
